Decomposing the “Kernel Gain”: What Actually Buys Accuracy on Handwritten Digits

CS5487 Machine Learning · Assignment 3 – Course Project Proposal · Group: [member 1, member 2, member 3] · 16 Nov 2026

1. Problem

The default task — 10-class handwritten digit classification on the provided 4,000-image MNIST subset (784-d pixel vectors) — comes with a useful reference point: 1-NN on raw pixels achieves 0.9135 / 0.9185 test accuracy on the two trials (mean 0.9160). Kernel methods are routinely reported to beat this, and the improvement is usually attributed to “nonlinearity”. But three things change at once when a linear classifier is replaced by an RBF SVM: the feature space (dimension), the shape of the decision boundary, and the effective regularisation. Our project isolates the middle one.

Research question. At a fixed feature space, how much does a nonlinear decision boundary actually buy over the best linear model, and how does that gain depend on the number of retained dimensions? At which dimension does the gain stabilise or vanish?

The answer is practically relevant: it tells a practitioner whether to spend effort on features or on the classifier, and it makes the usual “kernel methods are better” statement quantitative instead of anecdotal.

2. What we plan to do

AxisVariants (all transforms fitted on training data only)
Features raw pixels in [0,1]; per-pixel standardisation; PCA with k ∈ {10, 20, 30, 50, 80, 120, 200, 300, 500}, each with and without whitening.
Linear models BDR with shared covariance (LDA, with and without shrinkage on the pooled covariance); multinomial logistic regression; linear SVM.
Nonlinear models BDR with class-dependent full covariance (QDA, regularised); RBF-kernel SVM.
Reference 1-NN with Euclidean distance (reproduces the published baseline).

The two axes are fully crossed (10 feature spaces × 7 classifiers × 2 trials), so every classifier is seen in every feature space. This deliberately includes the regime where full-covariance BDR is ill-posed (p > nclass); those failures are reported as results, not silently dropped, because they are exactly what a practitioner needs to know.

3. Data, protocol and evaluation

  • Data. The provided digits4000 subset, used exactly as given. Each of the two trials has 2,000 training and 2,000 test images, and no writer appears in both, so the split measures generalisation across writers.
  • Model selection. All hyper-parameters (SVM C and γ, logistic C, shrinkage / reg_param) are chosen by 5-fold stratified cross-validation inside the training set. The test set is touched once per configuration; we will also report CV accuracy next to test accuracy to show the two agree (no test-set tuning).
  • Metrics. Per-trial accuracy and mean ± s.d. over the two trials; per-class recall and precision; full confusion matrices; the accuracy-versus-k curve; and the estimated marginal gain of nonlinearity, defined as (best nonlinear) − (best linear) at each k.
  • Tooling. Python + scikit-learn (third-party code, to be acknowledged in the report).

4. What counts as a good outcome

  • Beat the 1-NN baseline (0.9160) by at least 3 percentage points, with trial-to-trial s.d. ≤ 0.005 — i.e. the improvement must be larger than the split-to-split noise.
  • Locate the dimension at which the nonlinear gain stops changing, or show that it does not.
  • Give an evidence-backed account of which preprocessing helps, which hurts, and why — in particular, a mechanism for the whitening and per-pixel-standardisation effects, not just numbers.

5. Preliminary evidence that the project is feasible

A pilot run of the full cross (already completed, same protocol as above) reproduces the baseline exactly (0.9135 / 0.9185), and shows the effect is large enough to be measured: linear models saturate at ≈0.902 from k ≥ 50 onward, while RBF SVM holds ≈0.948 from k = 20 to 500, and regularised QDA peaks at 0.9548 (k = 30) before decaying. PCA-20 retains only 65% of the pixel variance yet already supports 0.947, so the discarded tail matters mainly for covariance estimation, not for class separation. Whitening never helps in our pilot and hurts badly at high k (1-NN drops from 0.918 to 0.617 at k = 200). These observations define the analysis to be completed; the remaining work is breadth (standardisation, parameter sensitivity, learning curves) and writing, not feasibility.
